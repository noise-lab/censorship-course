| 5 | **Three Families of Fingerprint** | • Figure: a torrent named "Lean On Me" (top) against the label's reference recording (bottom); the spectral envelopes line up peak for peak, and nothing reads the filename or lyrics<br>• Exact hash (PhotoDNA): same file after normalization. Perceptual hash: looks the same after resize, recolor, recompress. Spectral (Audible Magic, Content ID): sounds the same after re-encoding, trimming, renaming<br>• The platform stores the fingerprint, not the work, and compares against a reference library that rightsholders upload<br>• Read first: Microsoft on PhotoDNA, [microsoft.com/en-us/photodna](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/photodna) |
| 6 | **Inside an Audio Fingerprinter** | • Echoprint pipeline (The Echo Nest, open-sourced 2011): downsample to 11 kHz mono, whiten, split into subbands, find note onsets, hash the onset-timing pattern into short codes<br>• Lookup is an inverted index of codes plus a time-histogram vote<br>• The match threshold is a platform setting, not law; a cover with different timing, a parody, or a new recording does not match<br>• Read first: [Echoprint on GitHub](https://github.com/spotify/echoprint-codegen) |
| 7 | **What the Detector Cannot See** | • Diagram: upload, fingerprint, match against the reference library; yes means a claim, no means invisible<br>• The system answers one question, "have we seen this exact thing before"; no concept of fair use, context, or intent<br>• Two AI failure modes, the hinge of the lecture: a synthetic song has no reference and passes; a model that memorized a song emits something the detector catches as a copy of the original<br>• Blind in one direction, over-eager in the other; the platforms have started building something else (slide 16) |

| 9 | **Three Questions, Not One** | • Input: was training on the work lawful (fair use, licensed, pirated)? Inside: does the model contain a copy (memorization)? | 10 | **Question 1 in US Courts: Training** | • Read the holdings column slowly; each case turned on a different reason<br>• Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal., Jun 2025): training on books Anthropic bought was fair use; downloading millions of pirated books to keep as a library was not; $1.5B settlement for the piracy claim, final approval Jul 20 2026, about $3,000 per work, claims filed for about 91 percent of eligible works; final approval by Judge Martínez-Olguín, who took over from Alsup. Read first: [JURIST, Jul 22 2026](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/judge-approves-record-1-5-billion-settlement-involving-anthropic/)<br>• Kadrey v. Meta (N.D. Cal., Jun 2025): fair use because those thirteen authors put in no evidence that Llama hurt their sales; Chhabria wrote pages on how the next plaintiff could win on "market dilution". Read first: [Authors Alliance](https://authorsalliance.substack.com/p/meta-wins-on-fair-use-for-now-but)<br>• Thomson Reuters v. Ross (3d Cir., Sep 29 2026): headnotes are the one-paragraph editorial summaries a Westlaw editor writes above each opinion; Ross copied thousands to train a competing legal-search tool; not fair use, "no more than an ordinary copyright case"; first appellate ruling on AI training, and narrow (non-generative, direct competitor). Read first: [Copyright Lately](https://copyrightlately.com/thomson-reuters-v-ross-third-circuit/)<br>• NYT v. OpenAI (S.D.N.Y.): undecided; cross-motions for summary judgment on fair use, replies due Nov 20 2026; no ruling yet. Read first: [OpenAI's own page](https://openai.com/new-york-times/), for the defendant's framing |
| 11 | **Fair Use's Fourth Factor: Which Market Counts?** | • Fair use is the defense that excuses copying; four factors: purpose (transformative?), nature of the work, amount taken, effect on the market for the original<br>• All three decided cases turned on the fourth factor, and the judges disagreed about which market: Chhabria says license fees lost to a transformative use are "not cognizable" (counting them would make the factor circular) and the market the outputs flood is what counts; the Third Circuit says a "rapidly developing" licensing market for training data is real harm; Alsup says training is "quintessentially transformative" and the piracy is a separate wrong<br>• Same statute, same factor, three answers; the breakout motion lives here<br>• Course tie-in: if the licensing-market view wins, the right to train runs through catalogue owners, a gate on what models may learn (slide 18) |
| 12 | **Question 2 in Europe: Is the Model a Copy?** | • GEMA is Germany's music rights society, the ASCAP/BMI equivalent<br>• GEMA v. OpenAI (Munich, Nov 11 2025): ChatGPT recited Grönemeyer and Fischer lyrics verbatim; the court held the lyrics were memorized in the weights, a reproduction the text-and-data-mining exception does not cover; users are not to blame for simple prompts; under appeal. Munich repeated the rule for music in GEMA v. Suno (Jul 31 2026). Read first: [William Fry, Nov 12 2025](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/gema-v-openai-europes-direction-on-ai-infringement/)<br>• Getty v. Stability (London, Nov 4 2025): Stable Diffusion stores no copy, so it is not an "infringing copy"; permission to appeal granted Dec 2025. Read first: [Brabners](https://www.brabners.com/insights/intellectual-property/getty-images-v-stability-key-intellectual-property-lessons-for-ai-developers)<br>• A week apart, opposite results, both right on their facts: Munich had evidence of specific memorized works, London found no stored copies. The CJEU Advocate General in Like Company v. Google (Nov 3 2026) says in passing, since the training questions were inadmissible, that training is as a rule within the TDM exception; chatbot answers that recognisably reuse an article may infringe the press publisher's right<br>• The hands-on's experiment 1 is the GEMA test |
| 13 | **Question 3: Who Owns the Output?** | • Thaler v. Perlmutter: the D.C. Circuit (Mar 2025) upheld the Copyright Office's refusal to register an AI-made image; cert denied Mar 2 2026. Human authorship is required<br>• Copyright Office Part 2 (Jan 2025): prompts alone are not authorship; human selection, arrangement, and modification can be. Part 3 (May 2025, still pre-publication): training is "often transformative" but not automatically fair use; rejects the "it learns like a human" analogy; favors letting voluntary licensing develop without government intervention, with collective licensing only if the market fails. Read first: [copyright.gov/ai](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/)<br>• The responses asked whether an AI company can own the song its model made: no one can, unless a human did enough to count; that is why the money is all on the input side (next slide)<br>• A student's point: if meaningful human contribution is the test, much of today's software is on the wrong side of it |
| 14 | **Music: The Labels Settle, Then Sue Again** | • Suno and Udio make full songs from a text prompt; in June 2024 Universal, Sony, and Warner sued both for copying the labels' sound recordings to train the models<br>• Direction of the deals: the label licenses its catalogue to the service; the service pays, retrains on licensed material, and keeps outputs in a walled garden the label controls. Universal to Udio (Oct 29 2025), Warner to Udio (Nov 19 2025), Warner to Suno (Nov 25 2025; Suno also buys Songkick from Warner)<br>• Sony licensed nothing and sues on; Universal and Sony sued Suno again on Sep 18 2026 over 60,202 specific recordings; the Massachusetts schedule puts a US fair-use ruling in 2027<br>• The new front: the musicians' union sued Universal and Warner (Jun 5 2026) for licensing members' recordings into the deals without pay or disclosure, and a class of musicians sued Suno (Aug 31 2026) over right of publicity<br>• One-sentence version if short on time: the labels sued, two of three settled by licensing their catalogues, Sony sues on |
| 15 | **The Platforms Build Something Else** (section) | • Transition: the detector's answer to the "no reference" problem is a new kind of reference |
| 16 | **From Content ID to Content Identity** | • Say what Content ID is first: YouTube's copyright detector since 2007; rightsholders upload references, every upload is fingerprinted, a match becomes an automatic claim (monetize, track, or block); about 2.5 billion claims in 2025, 90 percent monetized rather than removed, under 1 percent disputed<br>• Likeness detection is the same machine with a library of people: enroll with a government ID and a selfie video; matches in AI-generated video come back as a list; the person chooses a privacy removal, a copyright claim, or nothing. Pilot 2025; politicians, officials, journalists in spring 2026; entertainers from Apr 21 2026, with feedback from CAA, UTA, WME. Read first: [Music Ally, Apr 24 2026](https://musically.com/2026/04/24/youtube-rolls-out-celebrity-ai-likeness-detection/); also [TechCrunch, Apr 21 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/21/youtube-expands-its-ai-likeness-detection-technology-to-celebrities/) and YouTube's own [AI page](https://www.youtube.com/howyoutubeworks/ai/)<br>• Also: a synthetic-singing detector inside Content ID in pilot with labels; uploader disclosure labels for realistic synthetic content; EU AI Act Article 50 transparency duties from Aug 2 2026<br>• C2PA content credentials: an industry standard for a signed record of how a file was made and edited, embedded in the file; present only if the creator signed. Read first: [contentcredentials.org](https://contentcredentials.org/)<br>• The phrase "Content ID to Content Identity" is from the [University of Baltimore Law Review, Mar 2026](https://ubaltlawreview.com/2026/03/youtubes-ai-likeness-detection-tool-and-the-emerging-law-of-digital-identity/) |
| 17 | **Two Detectors, Two Defaults** | • Walk the two rows top to bottom; the same three boxes, different third box<br>• Content ID acts first: the claim is automatic and the uploader's revenue moves before any person looks. Likeness detection waits: the match is a notification; nothing happens until the enrolled person asks<br>• The default is the policy: automatic enforcement over-removes and favors the claimant; a human in the loop under-removes and favors the speaker. YouTube chose the first for copyright and the second for faces<br>• Ask the room: which default for a deepfake of a candidate posted the week before an election (fast removal matters)? For a parody of a celebrity (fair use matters)? The answer flips, which is the point |
| 18 | **AI and Copyright as a Speech Lever** | • Four levers, one sentence each; dwell on 2 and 4<br>• 1 Claims at machine scale: generative tools make notices as cheap as content; same asymmetric counter-notice, longer windows of silence<br>• 2 "Made with AI" becomes a category to suppress: AI-assisted work has no owner to defend it (*Thaler*, Part 2); platforms must label or demote synthetic content (EU AI Act Art. 50, YouTube labels); publishers set AI-use rules for what they will print. A detector's false positives land on legitimate speech, as Content ID's do. The course book is the example: a university press deciding what AI assistance it accepts in a manuscript is deciding what gets published<br>• 3 Pre-emptive refusal: models already refuse whole categories (lyrics, long passages) as policy, shaped by litigation (Anthropic's Jan 2025 lyrics-guardrail stipulation in Concord v. Anthropic); GEMA adds a European legal reason; the suppression moves from the takedown to the model's policy, invisible to the user; experiment 1 shows it<br>• 4 Licensing as a gate on learning: if training needs a licence, catalogue owners decide what a model may learn, and what it never learned it cannot surface; the filter bubble at the training stage (Lecture 10), with the same measurement problem |
| 19 | **Section 230: Three Questions That Always Come Up** | • §230 (1996): a platform is not the publisher of what users post and may moderate without becoming one; copyright is the exception, governed by the DMCA<br>• Precedent: *Smith v. California* (1959), a bookseller cannot be convicted for an obscene book he had not read; *Stratton Oakmont v. Prodigy* (1995) made a forum that moderated liable as a publisher, which punished moderation; §230 was written the next year to reverse that<br>• Political neutrality: Texas HB 20 and Florida SB 7072 (2021) banned viewpoint-based removal; *Moody v. NetChoice* (2024) held curation is the platform's own protected speech and remanded for feature-by-feature analysis; in practice, is "anti-vaccine" a viewpoint or a policy violation, and which state's law governs a travelling user? Florida's $250,000-a-day fine for deplatforming a statewide candidate<br>• Common carriage: the net-neutrality argument, made for the pipes since the 2000s and now for the platforms; Tuesday's economic-controls lecture takes it up for the pipes<br>• Coda: cut first if time is short; it can go to Slack |
