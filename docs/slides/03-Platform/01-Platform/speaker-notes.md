|  | **Family 1: Fingerprinting (Exact / Perceptual Match)** | **PhotoDNA** (Microsoft, 2009) is the canonical example — see [Microsoft's PhotoDNA page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/photodna). |  | **Spectral Fingerprinting: Match the Sound, Not the File** | • **Figure:** recovered from the original pptx (slide 20). Top track is a torrent *named* "Lean On Me"; bottom is the reference fingerprint of Bill Withers' recording. The original animation swept a playhead across both; now walk it by hand: the two spectral envelopes line up peak for peak, so the system declares a match without reading the filename.<br><br>• **What to say:** the filename is a claim, the spectrum is evidence. Re-encoding, trimming, or renaming the file does not move the peaks, which is why this family is so hard to evade for *known* works.<br><br>• **Where it runs today:** Audible Magic for hosting sites, YouTube's Content ID for uploads — see the [YouTube Copyright Transparency Report](https://transparencyreport.google.com/youtube-policy/) for the scale of automated claims. Content ID is the largest exact-match moderation system in the world and is also the one with the longest history of fair-use disputes.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** database-bound and context-free. A cover, a parody, or an unreleased recording has no reference and is invisible; a licensed clip quoted under fair use matches perfectly and is taken down anyway. That asymmetry is the bridge to the ML-prediction family. |
|  | **Inside an Audio Fingerprinter: Echoprint** | • **Figure:** Echoprint pipeline diagram, recovered from the original pptx (slide 21); the deck's source is the Leiden Media Technology write-up: [wt2015_echoprint.pdf](http://mediatechnology.leiden.edu/images/uploads/docs/wt2015_echoprint.pdf). |  | **Perceptual vs. Cryptographic Hashing** | • **Figure:** embedded picture from the original pptx (slide 23); the deck's own notes credit the [bertolami.com post on perceptual hashing](https://bertolami.com/index.php?engine=blog&content=posts&detail=perceptual-hashing) (verified Oct 2026). Left: a cryptographic hash scatters neighbors; right: a perceptual hash keeps them adjacent.<br><br>• **What to say:** SHA-256 is designed so that one flipped pixel gives an unrelated digest; that is the wrong tool for "have we seen this picture?" A perceptual hash deliberately throws away the detail (greyscale, downsample, DCT, keep the low-frequency coefficients) so a resized or recompressed copy lands within a small Hamming distance of the original.<br><br>• **PhotoDNA** (Microsoft, 2009) is the canonical deployment — [Microsoft's PhotoDNA page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/photodna). Platforms store only hashes and compare against NCMEC's database, which is why the approach is both privacy-preserving and completely blind to novel material.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** the same locality that makes matching robust is the attack surface. Push the image just past the match threshold and it clears; the 2021 Apple NeuralHash collision demonstrations showed the reverse failure too, unrelated images hashing together. Thresholds are a policy choice, which foreshadows "Which AI You Pick Is the Policy." |
|  | **Case Study: GIFCT — One Hash, Every Platform** | The **Global Internet Forum to Counter Terrorism** (<https://gifct.org/>), founded 2017 by Facebook, Microsoft, Twitter, and YouTube, runs a **shared hash database** of terrorist/violent-extremist content: per the [2025 GIFCT Annual & Transparency Report](https://gifct.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2025-GIFCT-Annual-Report.pdf) (published May 21 2026; [summary page](https://gifct.org/2026/05/21/our-impact-in-2025/)), ≈**408,000** distinct items (329k images, 79k videos, 200 PDFs) and ≈**2.4M** hashes (+123,500 in 2025) across **39** member platforms, after a record seven joined in 2025 (TikTok, Snap, Roblox, GitHub, SoundCloud, Anthropic, 4D Hype), where one platform's takedown decision **propagates to all** members. This is the syllabus's "centralization = takedown risk, no appeal" case study: scope crept from ISIS imagery to broader "violent extremism" (**mission creep**), and *how* content is added, by whom, and what appeal exists stay largely opaque. Civil-liberties critics — Brennan Center, EDRi, Statewatch — flag the absence of any due-process review. The teaching point mirrors the earlier centralization slide: fingerprinting is precise, but a *shared* database turns one firm's judgment into a web-wide deletion.
