* **Discussion: how would you detect DNS manipulation, given that answers legitimately vary?**
    * Check whether the answer is plausibly nearby, using latency (`ping`) or the path (`traceroute`); a "local" answer that is far away is suspicious
    * Check consistency within one geography. Load balancing and failover still cause some change
    * Check who owns the returned address (`whois`)
    * Fetch the content and compare; personalization makes this unreliable
    * Compare the TLS certificate presented
    * None is perfect. In general this is hard, which is why measurement studies of DNS manipulation are difficult
    * DNSSEC was set aside for time
* **Encrypted DNS (DNS over HTTPS)**
    * By default your operating system sends unencrypted lookups to a local resolver supplied by the network you joined. Anyone on the path can see and alter them
    * DNS over HTTPS moves lookups into the browser and encrypts them to a chosen provider: Cloudflare by default in Firefox, Google in Chrome. Introduced roughly ten years ago by Mozilla and Cloudflare; turning it on by default was controversial
    * Benefit: on-path parties can no longer see or tamper with lookups
    * Cost: you must trust the resolver operator, and resolution that used to be spread across many networks is concentrated in a few companies, a new chokepoint
    * Usability: only two students had heard of it. The settings are hard to interpret, and an attempt in class to point the browser at a different public resolver did not work. Left as an exercise
* **Breakout: is centralized encrypted DNS a net gain or a net loss for user autonomy?**
    * For: encryption, and cases like Turkey where a large public resolver helped users get around local blocking
    * Against: one operator can refuse to resolve a name for everyone, and most users would never know why a page failed to load

* **TCP mechanics (for the questions asked)**
    * The three-way handshake: SYN (synchronize), SYN-ACK, ACK. Drawn as two machines, client on the left, server on the right
    * It is the Two Generals problem: nobody ever learns whether the final ACK arrived. The sender simply starts sending data. Teardown (FIN, acknowledgments in both directions) has the same property
    * A reset (RST) aborts a connection or refuses one. Normal uses: a SYN sent to a port with no listener gets a reset ("go away"); a killed process; any fast teardown
    * Forged resets: the Great Firewall watches for a SYN to a blocked destination and sends a reset to the client, which aborts regardless of what the server later replies
    * "Ignoring the Great Firewall of China" (Cambridge, 2006): if the client simply ignores resets, the connection proceeds. It worked because the firewall was stateless, sending a reset and forgetting the connection. The firewall later reset both ends (correction: the 2006 paper already reports resets sent to both ends)
    * Injecting a duplicate or a reset requires being **on the path**: a router or other equipment between client and server, which in practice means a government or an ISP. The forged packet must carry a plausible sequence number; sequence numbers are 32-bit, visible in the clear (TLS does not encrypt TCP headers), and because a reset is accepted anywhere inside the receiver's window they can be brute-forced if necessary (correction: 16-bit was said in class)
    * The economics: an on-path censor only has to win a race, sending its packet before the real reply, so the manipulation is cheap once the vantage point exists
* **Why transport-layer manipulation is becoming less attractive**
    * Encryption hides content, and shared hosting (one cloud or CDN address serving many sites) means an IP address no longer identifies a site
    * Censors answer with **website fingerprinting**: even encrypted, a page has a characteristic number of objects and sizes and a characteristic timing, which together act as a fingerprint of the site being visited
    * Questions from the room: oblivious DNS does not help here (it protects the lookup, not the transfer sizes); padding traffic with extra data does help and is the standard defense, with a large research literature behind it
    * Net: on-path manipulation needs a vantage point plus fingerprinting; friction, flooding, and platform-level controls are easier. Techniques for detecting TCP manipulation return in the measurement unit
* **Breakout B (short): is the cat-and-mouse game winnable?**
    * Prep-read tour: QUIC, a newer transport developed by Google and used by Chrome, encrypts more of the connection setup and can split a connection across paths, which raises new censorship questions. Tor's pluggable transports exist to protect the initial connection to the network, which is the sensitive moment for any VPN-like tool; each transport (obfs4, then Snowflake) gets fingerprinted in turn, and Russia recently blocked Snowflake. VPNs move the trust problem to the provider
    * Report-back: censors have many tools and take the path of least resistance, which can be economic or legal rather than technical. The asymmetry runs both ways: a circumventing user must hide every aspect of their traffic while the censor needs to spot one anomaly, but the censor must close every channel while the user needs one that works
    * Can traffic evade increasingly good machine-learning detection? Open question; flagged as a project direction (pit detection models against generative evasion). The role of AI in this race was deferred
* **DNSSEC (secure DNS, as distinct from encrypted DNS)**
    * The resolver walks the hierarchy: root, then the `.edu` servers, then the `uchicago.edu` servers; each step is a referral
    * With DNSSEC each referral is signed. A signature is made with a private key and checked with the matching public key; the referral carries the next zone's key, signed by the zone above it. This is **integrity**, not confidentiality: encrypted DNS hides queries, DNSSEC proves answers were not altered
    * The chain starts at the root key, which ships with the operating system or software. Same root-of-trust question as certificates: you have to start by trusting something
    * What it defends against: an on-path forged answer cannot carry a valid signature, so it is rejected. What it does not: a compromised root key, or a bad key added to the root store. Keys have been added to and removed from operating-system root stores over the years. This is a quieter form of consolidation: whoever holds a root key controls everything below it
    * Demo: the operating system's keychain lists the root certificates it trusts; most users never look
* **Hands-on: `dig` with DNSSEC**
    * `dig +dnssec` shows the signature records (RRSIG). `+trace` shows the full iteration from the root, with each referral, its signature, and a DS record pointing at the next zone's key
    * UChicago's domain is now signed, which it was not a year ago; so is a major news site (correction: a check on Oct 30 found cnn.com, nytimes.com, google.com, amazon.com, facebook.com, and wikipedia.org all unsigned; uchicago.edu is signed). Deployment is far broader than when the exercise was written. Task: find a domain that is not signed

    * **Why DNS rather than BGP?** DNS is easier to manipulate: operate a resolver and get people to use it, then return the wrong answer. It is also harder to detect, because DNS answers vary naturally. Routing manipulation is a sledgehammer: brute force, and it works if it works. For a complete shutdown (Egypt) withdrawing routes is the easier tool, and India uses it more often than most. In general DNS is manipulated far more than routing
* **Lecture: routing and route hijacks** (book 2.2.3)
    * The internet is a collection of independently operated networks, **autonomous systems**. BGP, the Border Gateway Protocol, is simply the routing protocol those networks use to talk to one another. Directions one hop at a time: a network gets your traffic as far as its border, then the next network takes over. Not a full route the way a map app gives one; the reason is scalability (nobody can run a shortest-path computation over the whole internet), so BGP adds a layer of abstraction
    * Security is very limited. Efforts to secure routing have largely failed except for the **Resource PKI** (RPKI). Recommended easy read: Sharon Goldberg, "Why Is It Taking So Long to Secure Internet Routing?" (Communications of the ACM, 2014), the source of the diagram shown
    * **Hijacks.** Any autonomous system can advertise any block of addresses to its neighbors, and routers mostly believe what they hear and pass it on. Mistakes cause outages (at least one of the recent large cloud-provider outages was a routing misconfiguration); malicious parties can do the same deliberately
    * **Prefix arithmetic, on the board.** An address is 32 bits, four 8-bit numbers. `128.135.24.0/24` means the first 24 bits are fixed and the last 8 are a wildcard: 2^8 = 256 addresses. A `/23` fixes 23 bits and covers 2^9 = 512 addresses, so a `/24` is *more specific* (a *longer prefix*) than a `/23`. The table shows overlapping prefixes like these
    * **A student asked:** if several routers advertise the same addresses, which one does a router pick? The **most specific (longest) prefix wins**. Among routes of equal length a set of rules decides, one of the higher-priority ones being the fewest autonomous systems on the path, which is correlated with distance but need not be
    * **Fighting back.** A hijack is not something a user sees easily: a withdrawn route is obvious (no internet), but traffic quietly detouring through another country may go unnoticed. Step one is detection: a network can watch public route collectors for someone else advertising its addresses. Step two: you cannot order the hijacker to stop, but you can split your own prefix in two and advertise the halves, which are more specific and so win. This works only up to a point: if everyone advertised ever-longer prefixes the table would grow exponentially, so many routers filter or ignore prefixes longer than `/24`
    * **Questions from the room.** Could you advertise half the internet with a `/2`? You could try; it would lose to every more specific route. Is there a law against hijacking? The instructor knows of no prosecution or case law; there are legal theories but nothing tested. Who can actually do this? You need a router that another network's router is willing to believe. The barrier is not in the protocol but in the setup: convincing an operator to connect to you and speak BGP. Small ISPs enter such arrangements all the time (a university routing for a local library, for example), so it is not far-fetched. The 1997 AS 7007 incident from the reading was caused by a very small ISP
    * **A student asked:** if BGP only gives step-by-step directions, how does anything know where to send traffic? Drawn on the board from a real table entry: UChicago's `128.135.0.0/16`, reached through an intermediate provider and then Internet2, the US academic research backbone. Each network on the path only knows how to get the packet to the next border; `whois` turns the autonomous-system numbers into names
    * **A student asked:** why does withdrawing a route work; can't routers cache the old one? In theory they could and sometimes do, but a cached route cannot tell a deliberate withdrawal from a genuine failure, and reacting to failures is the whole point. The course theme again: distinguishing a legitimate failure or glitch from an intentional act, whether a route, a DNS answer, a moderation decision, or (Tuesday's topic) slow traffic

* **Framing: degrading instead of blocking** (book 2.3.1)
    * Everything so far has been outright disruption: poisoned DNS answers, reset TCP connections, manipulated routes. Today's topic is subtler: make a service slow rather than unreachable. Many users give up, and there is no hard evidence that anything was blocked, because a deliberate slowdown is hard to tell from poor network conditions
    * Two places to degrade traffic, at opposite ends of the path. On the user's side, **throttling**: a shaper slows the sender, and it can be very selective (one application, part of one application, certain sites) and made to look like an ordinary slow network. On the service's side, a **denial-of-service attack** overwhelms a finite resource so the server itself looks down or slow. Example: China's attack on GitHub, which hosted circumvention tools promoted by GreatFire.org. Both look like performance problems, both disrupt availability, and neither leaves a clean pointer to the censor, which is the deniability the censor wants
    * Throttling (rate limiting) is a legitimate network-management tool that keeps one user or application from hogging shared capacity; the same mechanism becomes censorship depending on the target and intent. Molly Roberts calls this **friction**: raising the cost of reaching content until users quit
* **How a traffic shaper works**
    * Two steps: **identify** the traffic (which application, which destination), then **shape** it. Traffic is sorted into queues by service (video in one, everything else in another) and the shaper controls how fast each queue drains
    * Identification is an arms race with encryption. Three methods and their weaknesses: watch **DNS** lookups (for Netflix, say) and throttle the addresses that come back, which over-throttles when addresses are shared; read the domain name in the **TLS handshake** (the Server Name Indication), which Encrypted ClientHello now hides; or inspect **packet sizes, timings, and signatures** (deep packet inspection), which is costly and degrades as more traffic is encrypted. Breaking identification is therefore one of the better defenses against throttling
    * Encrypted ClientHello is deployed in current browsers, and censors have responded by blocking the TLS versions that carry it. Lesson: encrypting the identifier does not end the arms race; the censor targets whatever new pattern the defense creates
* **What the shaper actually does to your traffic** (the question nearly half the class asked in some form)
    * The leaky bucket and token bucket are **scheduling abstractions**, not devices that handle packets. A leaky bucket drips at a set rate; each drip tells the router to take one packet off the corresponding queue and send it, so the drip rate is the sending rate. There is typically one bucket per queue or per rate you want to enforce, and different queues can drain at different rates. A token bucket is a bank balance: tokens accrue at a set rate, and you can pull packets off the queue as long as you have tokens to spend, which allows bursts. Once the tokens are gone, nothing is sent and the buffer fills
    * What a user sees, in order. First, **higher delay**: the device serves the queue slowly, so packets wait in line. Then, if packets arrive faster than the queue drains, the queue fills and the device **drops packets**. Dropped packets mean lost data, and senders interpret loss as congestion and **slow themselves down** (TCP congestion control). The shaper itself drops nothing; it serves the queue slowly, the queue overflows, and the sender's own protocol finishes the job
    * Can a censor make this indistinguishable from congestion? Largely yes: a shaped service plan from your ISP looks exactly like this, and a congested network also produces higher delay and drops
    * **Bufferbloat** (asked in a response, to be defined in the book). Buffers sit in routers and exist because traffic is stochastic and bursty: everyone in a room may send at once or at different times, and the buffer absorbs that variation. Buffers that are too large produce huge delays with no drops, so nobody slows down; drops are the signal to slow down, and newer transport protocols also treat rising delay as a signal
    * Narrowly targeted throttling is harder to detect than slowing everyone down: the more focused and the more variable it is, the harder it is to see

A prefetching agent that pulls the pages you read into a local cache is a different thing: the set of sites a person actually consumes is small, storage is cheap, and an agent could fetch that slice of the web overnight. Throttling a site the user never visits live loses much of its effect

Soft, client-side paywalls (New York Times, Wall Street Journal) usually return the whole article and hide it with JavaScript, so a scraper or an agent gets the full text. * **Lecture: Denial of service** (book 2.3.2; this week's "crossover episode" with the Wednesday security class, kept short because the reading covers the mechanics)
    * Throttling slows things down at the client; denial of service slows the service for everybody. The focus here is denial of service launched by censors
    * Ways to deny service: saturate network capacity, or overload the server by exhausting its memory or CPU. A distributed attack recruits thousands of machines each sending a little traffic, focused on one victim like a magnifying glass focusing sunlight
    * Amplification: a small request produces a large reply, the classic case being a small DNS query to a resolver. Reflection: spoof the source address so the large reply lands on the victim instead of the attacker. Analogy: mailing a business a postcard with your enemy's return address so they receive the catalogs. Multiply by thousands of compromised machines
    * Reading-response questions answered in class
        * Is there a packet type that cannot cause denial of service? No. Anything with a finite resource (network capacity, CPU, memory, storage) can be exhausted. Example: a firewall installed to stop bad traffic becomes the target; exhaust its memory and everything behind it goes dark
        * Can a defense be designed so the defender never does more work than the attacker? That is the idea behind nearly every defense
        * Can packet captures tell a DDoS from ordinary congestion? Very hard
        * Has anyone run denial of service against a censor, given that deep packet inspection is expensive? The firewall point applies: an on-path censorship box that falls over takes down everyone behind it, so it is not obviously useful. The instructor called his own answer glib and the question a good one. A student asked whether a foreign adversary could cut China off by attacking the Great Firewall; in principle maybe, but how it is provisioned is not well understood
        * How hard is it to verify the source of traffic? An attacker who never needs the reply can lie about the source address. Egress filtering: the router at the edge of a network such as UChicago's drops outgoing packets whose source is not in its own address block. It relies on every network being a good citizen and costs money, so many do not do it, and once spoofed traffic leaves the leaf network it is very hard to filter. Ingress filtering (drop incoming traffic that claims to come from our own addresses) is somewhat more effective but does not protect against other spoofed addresses
        * A student asked whether any regulation requires such filtering. The instructor knows of none from governments; it exists as a published best common practice
    * The Great Cannon (about ten years ago, named by the researchers who documented it). The Great Firewall's usual move is to sniff traffic and inject TCP resets. The Great Cannon instead injected JavaScript into responses to users outside China who loaded content from Baidu; the injected script made those browsers send requests to GitHub, which hosted circumvention projects, and to GreatFire.org. The censor recruited outside browsers as its attack machines
    * Not covered: SYN floods and SYN cookies, Mirai and the Dyn outage, and the defenses slides. The security-course lecture covers the first two for students in both courses
* **Project Galileo**
    * The standard defense against volumetric attack is more capacity: host the site in more places or with more bandwidth, which costs money. Hypothetical: a university site such as a free-expression forum is a plausible target and is not provisioned for terabits per second
    * Cloudflare, a content delivery network, started Project Galileo in 2014: if Cloudflare decides a site is worthy and it is under attack, it gets denial-of-service protection for free. The question is who decides

* **Student presentation: personalization and filter bubbles** (first presentation of the term; slides are linked from the sign-up sheet)
    * Personalization is a continuous loop: collect profile data (age, gender, location) and behavioral data (searches, clicks, engagement); predict what content is most relevant and engaging; push it; measure engagement; re-rank and repeat
    * Personalization is the mechanism; a filter bubble is a possible outcome, in which a person sees a narrow range of information, content that reinforces existing beliefs keeps arriving, and opposing views become rare or absent
    * History: 1990s information filtering (the 1992 Tapestry system, which let users filter documents using other users' annotations; the mid-1990s MIT Media Lab idea of a personalized daily newspaper; Amazon's 1998 cookie-based recommendations); 2000s mass personalization by Google, Netflix, and Amazon, including Google's personalized search for all users in 2009; Eli Pariser's 2011 book and talk that popularized "filter bubble"; TikTok's 2016 shift to discovery driven by what you watch rather than whom you follow; and 2020s generative-AI personalization. What changed: the goal moved from managing information overload with explicit feedback to shaping what people encounter in order to optimize engagement metrics, pushing content people never sought out and now generating it
    * Open debates the presenters listed: whether filter bubbles are overstated; whether personalization causes polarization and radicalization or only reflects divisions already in society; whether it empowers users or undermines their agency; whether platforms should be regulated to prevent bubbles; and whether platforms should show people content they do not want to see
    * Class vote by show of hands: who is more responsible for what you see, the algorithm or the user (the tally was not captured)
    * Case study: the 2018–2019 narrative that YouTube's recommender pushed casual viewers toward extreme content
        * A 2020 audit of "radicalization pathways" grouped channels into mainstream media, the intellectual dark web, alt-lite, and alt-right, used commenting on a channel's videos as a proxy for exposure, and simulated users following successive recommendations. About 12 percent of users who commented only on intellectual-dark-web videos in 2017 commented on alt-right videos by 2018; roughly 40 percent of 2018 alt-right commenters could be traced to cohorts that had commented only on the two milder groups; about 4 percent of random walks starting from alt-lite channels and 1.5 percent starting from the intellectual dark web reached alt-right channels within five recommendation steps. Pathways from milder to more extreme content exist and recommendations help people find extremist channels, but commenting is an imperfect proxy and the causal role of the recommender is hard to establish
        * YouTube's 2019 change: recommend fewer borderline and misinformation videos (miracle cures, flat earth), which remain searchable; external reviewers rate videos against the guidelines and classifiers are trained on those ratings. YouTube reported a 70 percent drop in watch time of borderline content recommended from channels the viewer had not subscribed to
        * A later counterfactual-bot study: pairs of bots started from the same real users' watch histories, one continuing to follow what the human actually watched and the other following YouTube's recommendations. The bots following recommendations watched less far-right content than the bots replaying human choices. When a user switched from far-right to moderate videos, far-right recommendations faded after about thirty videos, while the human's own choices changed more slowly. The presenters' conclusion: user choice mattered more than the recommender for who ended up on far-right content, and on average the algorithm recommended less extreme content than people chose for themselves
        * Discussion questions the presenters posed: if people mostly choose this content themselves, does that let platforms off the hook; and how can one tell whether an article is organic or planted. One student observed that the selection bias runs both ways: creators make what they think the algorithm will favor, so content the algorithm does not favor is never produced to be shown, much as search results fill with AI-generated articles. * **Lecture: Disinformation** (book 3.5; slides are still a work in progress)
    * **What counts.** Disinformation is nonaccidentally misleading information (Fallis). Three conditions: it must carry information; it must create false beliefs; and it must do so intentionally. Misstating a fact you simply got wrong (the instructor's example: saying the Civil War started in 1962) is not disinformation; the term of art for accidentally wrong information is misinformation. Satire is also excluded. One-sided presentation is not disinformation either: a politician who backs a claim with a factoid stripped of context is not strictly propagating false information, though this is a gray area, and many of the sites in the hands-on are political. A student offered an example of something intended to create false beliefs that carries almost no information, which the instructor called a better example than their own; the example itself was not captured
    * **Is it illegal?** In a few narrow contexts, yes: defamation (false statements that damage a person) and securities fraud (false statements that move a stock price; the instructor recalled Elon Musk getting into trouble with regulators over posts about stocks). Political speech is protected in the United States even when false; the instructor pointed to *United States v. Alvarez* as the case to read, with the caveat that they are not a lawyer and would check further. In Europe the Digital Services Act regulates the platforms and their systems, not the content of what is said
    * **The consequence of leaving it to platforms.** If disinformation is not illegal in general, platforms end up deciding what is allowed. Their policies are not consistent with each other (a hands-on next week compares the content-moderation policies of different platforms), and even if they were, enforcing them consistently is of unclear feasibility, and auditing a platform for consistent enforcement is very hard (a research problem the instructor has thought about for a decade). Perceived inconsistency in policy or enforcement produces distrust: the sense that Facebook leans one way, X another, and that a chatbot like Claude has a political bent of its own. It is the same issue as Tuesday's demo, where two people got different answers and nobody could say whether the difference was benign personalization or something more insidious
    * **Pizzagate.** Nearly everyone had heard of it, unlike last year's class. A student told the story: stories spun out of the 2016 Clinton campaign email leaks claimed that Hillary Clinton and the Democratic Party were linked to a child-trafficking ring run from a pizza parlor in Washington, DC; a man showed up at the restaurant to confront the supposed ring and was apprehended before anyone was hurt. Much of the content was produced by people in Macedonia who, by their own account, had no interest in the outcome of the 2016 election; they had discovered that lurid stories about a presidential candidate drew clicks and ad revenue, and they made a lot of money (correction: Pizzagate began with U.S. users on Twitter, 4chan, and Reddit; the Macedonian sites in Veles are a separate, profit-driven story from the same election)
    * **Effects** (from Benkler, Faris, and Roberts, *Network Propaganda*, 2018, which the instructor said has aged well). Three effects of propaganda:
        * Induced misperception: people believe false things. This is the first-order effect everyone thinks about
        * Disorientation: people stop knowing what to believe and distrust sources, including sources they used to consider reputable; cynicism, the inability to tell truth from non-truth. This is the more insidious second-order effect: even if only a small group believes a particular false story, a much larger group spends brain cycles wondering whether to trust anything, including the platform itself ("I heard Facebook is a right-wing thing; should I believe what I read there?")
        * Distraction: change the subject. This follows directly from flooding; the instructor cited the current president as a master of changing the subject when pinned down
    * **Scale is what is new.** Orientations toward outlets (the Times as left-leaning, Breitbart as right-leaning, and so on) existed long before social media and could already disorient a reader. What changed is that the number of sources used to be countable on your hands and feet, and AI has made flooding so much cheaper that disorientation and distraction are far more wide-ranging. A further twist, raised by a student that day and suggested as a project topic: when you ask a chatbot a question, it reads a handful of websites and hands you a synthesis without necessarily telling you which sites, so you can no longer consider the source yourself. The instructor asked whether there is sociological work measuring these second-order effects and said they have not followed that literature closely
    * **Vocabulary.** Sock puppets: fake accounts (bots) that create or amplify content. Astroturfing: a fake grassroots movement, manufactured consensus. The word comes from AstroTurf, the artificial grass invented for the Houston Astrodome after real grass died under the dome's roof panels. Bogus reviews. Search manipulation and polluting a user's profile, which changes what that user is shown in the future; a cross-site request forgery attack can do exactly that
    * **A taxonomy of manipulation.** The instructor's own grid, built more than ten years ago and motivated by Cialdini's *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (a psychology book turned pop business classic about how marketers and persuaders steer choices; correction: it was published in 1984, not the 1970s). Three levers a manipulator can pull, the choice set (correction: this was attributed to Cialdini in class, but it is not one of his principles; the grid draws on him for social proof), the presentation, and the content, crossed with three stages of the information supply chain: production (newsrooms), dissemination (social networks), and discovery (search)
        * Choice set at production: what a newsroom chooses not to report. The instructor looks at the front pages of the Wall Street Journal and the New York Times daily and they are strikingly different; decisions are being made about what gets covered, presumably driven by clicks and money. A social network removing content and a search engine removing results are the more obvious cases (search comes in a later class)
        * Presentation: the same story is at the top of the Journal and three scrolls down in the Times. Layout and ordering are editorial decisions too
        * Content: a student noted that two outlets can frame the same set of facts in completely different ways, and that what one reader sees differs from what another sees. The instructor asked for the links and said the table is overdue for a refresh

* **Lecture: AI and copyright** (compressed to about fifteen minutes; the hands-on and the breakout were not reached and are tabled for a later class)
    * Pre-AI enforcement rested on two assumptions: a human made the uploaded thing, and there is an original somewhere to compare it against. Even then the line was never clean (sampling, remixing, interpolation in hip hop and pop), though a performance of a copyrighted work was clearly a use. Generative AI breaks both assumptions
    * Three ways to match an upload against a library: an exact (cryptographic) hash, which any crop or resize defeats; a perceptual hash for images, "does it look the same"; and a spectral fingerprint for audio, built from a spectrogram of which frequencies are prominent over time, which survives trimming and excerpting. Open-source fingerprinters such as EchoPrint are not robust to a cover version. An AI-generated song has no reference in any library, so it passes
    * Three questions, not one: was training lawful (fair use and its four factors); does the model contain a copy; who owns the output and does it infringe

Meta (fair use, largely because the plaintiffs did not show harm to their sales, the market-effect factor); Thomson Reuters v. Ross went the other way (copying Westlaw's editorial headnotes to train a competing legal search tool was not fair use)
    * Inside the model: a European court found that a chatbot reciting song lyrics on request meant the model stored them; the Stable Diffusion image-model case found no copy stored
    * Output: the Supreme Court declined to review the case of an AI-generated image refused copyright because no human made it (correction: "denied certification" was said in class; the Court denied certiorari, leaving the refusal in place). * **Student presentation 6: copyright, laws, and regulation**
    * Copyright basics: protection for original creative expression (writing, music, film, photos, illustrations, software). It protects the expression, not the facts or the subject: two photos of the same sunset, two articles on the same news. The rights holder's exclusive rights are reproduction, distribution, adaptation, public performance, and public display.
    * Fair use and its four factors, worked through a movie review that shows a short clip: the purpose of the use, the nature of the work, the amount and importance of what was used, and the effect on the market.
    * Enforcement online: notice and takedown with counter-notification, and automated matching such as YouTube's Content ID against rights holders' reference files. The connection to censorship: over-removal of lawful posts, misuse of claims to remove criticism, and the asymmetry that takedown is fast while restoration takes time and effort from the poster, so a timely video may be moot by the time it is restored.
    * History of US copyright
        * The Statute of Anne (1710), passed in response to the printing press and to break publishers' perpetual rights; the constitutional clause granting exclusive rights for limited times to promote progress.
        * The 1790 Act: maps, charts, and books; 14 years plus a 14-year renewal; registration with a district court required. 1831: music added, initial term 28 years.
        * Wheaton v. Peters (1834): the class voted, and the Court ruled against Wheaton, because there was no common-law copyright after publication and he had not registered, and because government-produced public documents cannot be copyrighted.
        * 1870: registration centralized in the Library of Congress. The 1891 Chace Act extended protection to foreign authors on a reciprocal basis, with a domestic-printing requirement. The Copyright Office was established in 1897.
        * The 1909 Act: a 28-year renewal for a 56-year maximum, notice instead of registration for published works, unpublished works left to state law, and the compulsory mechanical license for music.
        * The 1976 Act: one federal system; life plus 50 years, or 75 or 100 years for works made for hire; protection from the moment of fixation; termination of transfers after 35 years; and the safety valves of fair use, first sale, and library and archive exceptions.
        * Berne Convention (1989): no notice required. The DMCA (1998): anti-circumvention of technical protection measures, the triennial exemption rulemaking as its escape valve, and notice and takedown, which the presenters called the first point where platforms' incentive to take down quickly and restore slowly entered the law. The CASE Act's Copyright Claims Board as a low-cost alternative to federal court.
        * Today almost everything fixed in tangible form is protected unless it is government-made, released under an open license, or old enough to be in the public domain.
    * Pair discussion on how copyright should change in the AI era. One report-back asked whether AI output that visibly draws on an original is a derivative work the original author can control; the presenters said derivative-work rules for AI are still being worked out.
    * Case studies, as the presenters reported them
        * Cox v. Sony (Supreme Court, 2026): an ISP is contributorily liable only if it induced infringement or offers a service tailored to it. The record labels had sent Cox more than 160,000 notices over two years and Cox had terminated about 30 subscribers; the jury verdict had been about 1 billion dollars. The presenters contrasted the unanimous decision with the 5 to 4 Sony v. Universal (1984) staple-article-of-commerce doctrine it builds on: a product capable of substantial non-infringing uses is judged by its design, not by knowledge that some buyers will misuse it. The Court also noted the over-blocking risk of cutting off a whole address on accusation.
        * After Cox, plaintiffs began dropping contributory claims (the New York Times against OpenAI, keeping one against Microsoft; the music publishers against Anthropic), so the AI fights move to direct infringement and fair use.
        * Quiz: of more than 150 AI copyright suits, how many appellate rulings on generative-AI training exist? Zero. The only training case on appeal, Thomson Reuters v. Ross, involves a non-generative legal-research tool and was found not to be fair use.
        * Courts split AI into three stages: acquisition, training (mostly found to be fair use so far: Bartz v. Anthropic "exceedingly transformative," Kadrey v. Meta "highly transformative"), and output, where there is little law yet. Bartz settled for 1.5 billion dollars, about 3,000 dollars per work against a statutory range of 750 to 30,000, with the first payouts this week. Pending: New York Times v. OpenAI and Concord v. * **Measuring technical controls** (book 5.2)
    * Two themes from the reading responses ran through the lecture: what the measurements can and cannot tell you, and why measure at all, who the data is for.
    * Why it is hard: effective censorship is designed to look like failure. A measurement shows that something is unreachable or slow; it does not say why.
    * Why measure, and who it is for
        * To design better circumvention: knowing which blocking techniques a censor uses lets you improve circumvention and assess whether it works.
        * A student asked whether publishing detection methods lets censors adapt without anyone noticing. Yes: if a paper or source code says how DNS blocking or throttling is detected, the censor can evade the detection. That is mostly theoretical for repressive regimes but very real in net neutrality, where measurement tools have recognizable signatures and ISPs have been caught detecting the tool's traffic and prioritizing it. References to follow on Slack.
        * Variation within a country. Political scientists have asked for a decade whether controls vary by region (for example, whether provinces with different politics in China see different blocking). The instructor has not seen an authoritative study. A postdoc in the lab looked for regional variation in the OONI and GreatFire data a few years ago and found inconsistencies, but could not tell geographic variation from something else. The same question could be asked of the United States. Still open.
        * Variation across regimes. Do more authoritarian governments block more because the consequences differ from a democracy, where blocking would be perceived and reported differently? Classifying regimes and mapping them to the controls they use was posed as a study nearly ten years ago and was untenable then because the data did not exist; the data synthesis it needs is more tenable now, and the instructor suggested it as a project direction.
        * Correlating events. PhD students in the lab are correlating public outage reports and trouble tickets from providers (Cloudflare, Amazon, Microsoft, which had an outage today) with current events, weather, software upgrades, and elections. OONI has done this piecemeal in case studies, such as an outage lined up with an election; an exhaustive study is now feasible.
        * Diffusion. China has been treated as the thought leader in repressing speech since the Great Firewall in the late 1990s, and things that "would never happen in a Western democracy" have turned up there twenty years later. Whether techniques spread from repressive regimes to others is a hypothesis this data could test.
    * Why it looks like something else: censorship can be made to look like congestion, a network problem, or an outage. Today's campus email outage was the example: the first instinct was to report a local problem, and only the news said it was the provider. It can also be intermittent and localized. If it hit ten percent of people and sort of worked, it would be hard to tell there was an event at all, hard to be in the right place at the right time to measure it, and hard to attribute motive even with a measurement in hand.
    * The measurement ecosystem
        * OONI: volunteers install the probe through campaigns; the Explorer site shows what it collects.
        * Censored Planet: mostly virtual private servers inside the country, running measurements of every blocking type the course has covered. Measuring from data centers is not the same as measuring from phones or homes, but it runs around the clock.
        * IODA: watches Internet routing data, the kind examined earlier in the term, for disruptions.
        * Cloudflare Radar: measures traffic to Cloudflare's own infrastructure.
        * GFWatch: measures into China for reachability and replies.
        * Platforms measure too, which is Tuesday's topic: Google's transparency report shows traffic to its services dipping when a country censors them.
    * DNS manipulation. The baseline test is the one from the earlier hands-on: resolve a name, compare the address with someone else's, and notice that the answer changes when the VPN moves you. A different answer may be load balancing or geolocation rather than manipulation, so the hard problem is telling those apart; the papers in the reading give consistency checks for it.
    * TCP/IP filtering, at a high level only: a side channel. From outside you can probe the IP ID counter on a router and force it to increment; if your traffic is blocked, the counter does not increment, and you can observe that. The mechanics are more involved and were not covered. A student asked whether this works for newer versions of IP. It does not: IP ID is an IPv4 field, so the book's technique has a blind spot for IPv6.
    * Web filtering and Encore. Built in the instructor's lab about ten years ago to measure web filtering without recruiting anyone to install software. JavaScript embedded in a page the researchers controlled made every visiting browser request an object from a third-party site under test, the way a tracker works, except that the third party is the measurement target. The same-origin policy (a confluence with the security course) keeps the page from reading the cross-origin response, but JavaScript's load and error callbacks reveal whether the fetch succeeded, which is the side channel. You learn that a fetch failed, not why; many failures from one domain suggest the domain is blocked, and specific URLs can be tested the same way. IP-based blocking is less relevant now because of shared hosting.
    * Not covered today: the consistency metrics in detail, the mechanics of the idle scan and forward versus reverse blocking, the Kazakhstan interception case, and transparency reports and the DSA, which move to Tuesday.

* **Student presentation 7: AI as a censor**
    * Framing: every censorship decision reduces to allow or not. Should an email reach the inbox or go to spam; should a post be shown. AI here means systems that learn patterns from data rather than following hand-written rules, in three generations: classical machine learning, neural networks, and generative models. AI plays other roles on platforms (flooding, feed personalization); the talk was about AI as the classifier that decides. Intentional censorship (an authority or adversary deciding what is shown, the Great Firewall) was distinguished from unintentional over-removal by the systems themselves.
    * The framework: censorship as classification. Precision, false positives, and false negatives are the quantities, and the trade-off between them is where policy enters. The acceptable false-positive rate is a policy question, and at a billion posts a day one percent is ten million posts.
    * Content parsing and keyword blocklists: rigid, and you have to know what you are looking for. Naive Bayes spam filters compare the distribution of words in a message with known spam. Evasion is cheap: paste Shakespeare under the message, swap in look-alike characters, or put the text in an image. It is a losing battle because changing content costs the spammer less than building the filter costs the defender. The class tried it: everyone submitted a comma-separated blocklist against a corpus of spam and legitimate email and saw how many it got right on a leaderboard.
    * Classical machine learning: with most web traffic under TLS the censor cannot read the query, so the features are network-level properties (packet counts, sizes, timing) that are harder to forge, fed to supervised models. The same techniques power national-scale censors, anti-DDoS services, and intrusion detection. Defenses and evasion: decoy routing (Slitheen, 2016) hides censored traffic inside a real session with an allowed site by swapping out leaf content such as images; bot detection scores an automated browser as certainly a bot, and the TA's research passed it by simulating human timing and clicks from empirical distributions. The adversarial-machine-learning view: evasion at inference time (shape a circumvention tool's packet sizes and timing to look like video streaming), jailbreaking and prompt injection when a censor uses a language model to read pages, and data poisoning of the censor's training data when the model is updated. Takeaway: an arms race.
    * Neural networks: layers of computation, with the features learned by the model instead of engineered by a person. That scales, and it captures relationships a person could not specify, such as the meaning of a string of characters, or edges, shapes, and objects in an image. Great Firewall examples: images of a cartoon character used to mock a political leader, and images of river crabs, a homophone for "harmony" that people posted to mark content that had been harmonized away, until the crab images were censored too. Neural networks also sharpen deep packet inspection: they can pick up the small timing differences between a circumvention tool and human traffic. The cost of learned features is bias: a bank loan model that was never given race or gender recovered them from correlated features, and one group saw a false-negative rate 31 percent higher than another.
    * Generative models and scale: neural networks are expensive and language models far more so. The class guessed China's peak international throughput; the answer given was about ten terabytes per second, so a language model cannot sit on the path. Instead, language models generate and label training data for the smaller classifiers that do the real-time work.
    * The closer, a race against a language model classifying tweets as spam or not (a practice round of three, then the race), ran out of class time and was run after class for those who stayed. The backend was a single cloud instance set up by an agent for a few cents.
